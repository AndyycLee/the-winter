Sarah was nine the first time she learned what her excitement was worth to other people.

It was a blythe doll, a cheap plastic thing she'd found at the bottom of a bargain bin, but the melody inside it undid her completely — some twisted, cute waltz that made her chest ache in a way she didn't have words for yet. She played with it every night for two weeks. She had never owned a doll before, her family being too poor to afford such luxuries. She drew pictures of what she thought her friends looked like, and played songs with it, and a lot of blue.

At school, during show-and-tell, she brought it in.

She remembers dancing the doll with both hands because her fingers were still too small to get a good grip. She remembers the room going quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when it isn't interested but is being polite about it. Someone in the back laughed — not cruelly, just reflexively, the way children laugh at anything unfamiliar — and by the time the melody finished playing, Sarah understood, without anyone telling her directly, that she had shown them something private and gotten nothing back for it.

She smiled anyway. She said "isn't it silly" before anyone else could say it for her, because it hurt less coming from her own mouth.

That night, when her mother asked how show-and-tell went, Sarah said it went great. Everyone loved it, she said. She never played the doll at school again, though she kept it — it lived at the bottom of a drawer for years, wound down, silent, taken out only late at night when there was no one left to disappoint.

It wasn't a single lesson learned in a single day. It was more like a habit that calcified slowly, scene by scene: figure out what people want to hear before they finish asking, agree faster than you actually agree, smile before the disappointment has a chance to show on your face. By middle school it was no longer a strategy — it was just how her face worked. She could not remember, most days, what her own opinion about something felt like before she'd already adjusted it to fit the room.

The strange part was that no one around her was unkind. Her mother loved her, in the checked-out way of someone managing three things at once. Her classmates weren't cruel, just uninterested. There was no villain to resent, which somehow made it worse — she couldn't even be angry about it. It was just weather. It was just how things were.
