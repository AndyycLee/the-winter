The bus takes the winding, downward right turn into the valley, travels straight, and makes the second, upward right turn.

It always does. That part never changes.

The doors sigh open. A crowd of schoolboys and girls spills out into a yard washed grey with early light, and somewhere in the noise a voice calls out — small, insistent, aimed at no one in particular and yet unmistakably aimed.

This time, no one answers it.

The yard empties. The faceless crowd thins to nothing, the way smoke thins, and what's left standing there is a girl who has stopped expecting anyone to come.

◇

She has been sitting on the bridge for eleven minutes. She knows because she counted them, badly, the way you count things when you're trying not to think about what you're actually doing.

Below her the water moves the color of an old bruise. The fence behind her has a gap in it, low and easy, worn soft by whoever came through before her — kids, probably, looking for somewhere to smoke where no one's mother could find them. She had found the gap by accident on a walk she didn't remember deciding to take, three towns from the one she grew up in, in a season she can no longer place with any confidence. Spring, maybe. Everything that year runs together like water through a fist.

She had been good at something, once. Now she isn't sure the word "good" applies to her at all. There had been a school, white halls, the smell of antiseptic and overcooked coffee, a professor who said her hands were steady in a way that mattered. She doesn't remember deciding to leave that either. Decisions like that don't announce themselves. You simply wake up one day already gone.

Rock bottom, she thinks, testing the phrase the way you test a loose tooth. It sounds like something from a film. It does not feel like a location. It feels like a texture — the underside of her own ribs, worn smooth from being touched too often.

A voice, behind her.

"It's a good spot, huh."

Not a question. She doesn't turn right away. When she does, there's a boy — younger than her, she can tell immediately by something in the set of his shoulders, the way he hasn't finished growing into his own height yet — holding a can of something cheap, condensation sliding down onto his knuckles. He isn't looking at her like she's a problem to be solved. He's looking at the water.

"You're not going to try and talk me down," she says. It isn't quite a question either.

"Should I be?"

"I don't know. Everyone else would."

He shrugs, and sits, an arm's length away, close enough that she can hear the can hiss when he opens it, far enough that it doesn't feel like a claim on anything. "I just liked the view," he says. "You can see three bridges from here if you look far enough down the river. I counted once."

She almost laughs. It surprises her more than it should.

◇

They do not become anything so simple as friends. There isn't time, or there is time but neither of them knows how to spend it on something that ordinary. He is a grade behind her, in a school she doesn't attend anymore, in a life that is still mostly ahead of him instead of mostly behind. She tells him, once, that this is the trouble with people who meet too late — not too late in years, but too late in shape. He has room left to become something. She has already been carved down to whatever this is.

He doesn't argue with her about it. That, more than anything he says, is what she comes back for.

They meet at the bridge on the evenings she can't stand her own apartment, which is most evenings. He brings the cheap beer sometimes, though she never sees him drink more than half of one, like it's a prop rather than a habit. She talks, when she talks at all, about the hospital corridors she isn't walking anymore, about a mother who calls twice a week and says nothing worth answering, about the particular quiet of a life that emptied out faster than she filled it.

He listens the way some people breathe — like it costs him nothing, like it's simply what a body does.

Once, in early summer, she asks him why he comes back. He looks at the water for a long time before he answers.

"Because you're the only variable that isn't fixed," he says.

She doesn't fully understand what he means. She thinks, now, that she was supposed to ask him to explain it. She never does. There is always a next time, until the season she stops believing that, and by then the words have already slipped past the place where she could have caught them.

◇

The grade catches up to both of them eventually — his classes resume in a building she no longer has any reason to enter, and the days that once bent naturally toward the bridge stop bending. She tells herself it doesn't matter. She has told herself worse and believed it further.

It is autumn again, or something close to it, the last stubborn leaves letting go of the railing where she used to set her drink. She comes back to the bridge out of habit more than hope, the way you return to a room after the person who made it feel like somewhere has already left it.

He is there. She hadn't expected that.

He doesn't say anything about the months. Neither does she. He just moves over, the way he did the first time, leaving exactly enough space for her to sit if she wants to.

The water is the color of a bruise, still, or a bruise healing, she can't tell which color means which anymore.

His hand finds the railing first. Hers finds his.

The world does the thing it always does at the edges of things like this — the glass of the sky, the grey concrete, the low bruised water, all of it bleeding at once into a color too bright to be called anything but violent, and underneath it, so quiet it could be mistaken for nothing at all, something in her that has been silent for a very long time says, clearly, for the first time —

I don't want this to be the last time I ask.

And then, as it always does, it snaps.

That's a great instinct, and it's exactly the device that makes Kimi no Hanashi structurally interesting rather than just a twist-ending story — the past isn't withheld until the epilogue, it's given its own chapter, but delivered in a way that doesn't announce itself as "the truth" yet. The trick is disguise, not concealment.

Placement: I'd put it around chapter 5–6 of your outline (right where I suggested a "new dream fragment" earlier) — early-mid, when the reader still fully believes in the bus dream as his recurring dream and nothing more. That's the ideal moment to smuggle in the past life, because the reader's guard is down.

POV/voice — this is the key technique. Don't narrate it in his first-person "I" the way the rest of the book is told. Shift it. A few options, in order of how strongly I'd recommend them:

Third person, unnamed — "the boy," "the girl." No names, no "I." This distances it just enough that a reader clocks it as different without necessarily clocking it as real. It reads like a dream being described from outside, or like folklore — which fits your in-yeon theme perfectly (the narrator literally called it "a comfortable superstition" in ch2 — so give the reader a chapter that feels like superstition made concrete).
Present tense, contrasting with your past-tense main narration. Present tense already reads as dreamlike/timeless in fiction — readers will register it as "the dream, but longer" rather than "a flashback to a real past."
Structurally frame it as an extension of the existing bus dream, not a break from it — open with the same bus, same faceless crowd, same imagery from chapter 1, and only then let it diverge into new, specific details (cheap beer, the fence gap, nursing school). That continuity is what disguises it. The reader thinks "oh, we're back in the recurring dream" and doesn't realize you've quietly swapped whose dream it is.
What to include, what to withhold: Give the reader the textures from her letter — the cheap beer, "it's a good spot huh," the fence and bushes, nursing school, rock bottom — because these will land as devastating callbacks once the letter recontextualizes them. But cut before the deaths, or render them so obliquely they don't register as deaths on a first read
