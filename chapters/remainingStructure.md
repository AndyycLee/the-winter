Good — now that the mechanism is fixed, you can plant clues honestly instead of vaguely. Here's how I'd map the remaining chapters, keeping to Miaki's pacing: short chapters, each built around one image or one small scene, dream-fragments doing the lore-work so the ending letter only needs to *confirm*, not *explain*.

One structural decision first: **does the narrator ever get the full picture before she dies, or only after, in the letter?** I'd strongly recommend he never gets it — he gets fragments he explains away (same as ch1–2), and only the letter assembles them. That preserves the dramatic irony that makes the ending land. The reader, though, should be a half-step ahead of him by the final act — noticing what he won't let himself notice.

**Ch 4 — establishing the relationship, present tense**
Give them an actual scene together that isn't the bridge — the store, maybe, or a walk. Let her be a real, slightly annoying, funny person (per my earlier note — she needs specificity beyond the blue eye and cigarette). Plant one piece of impossible knowledge, deniable: she says something about him that she couldn't know (references a route, a habit, something from childhood) and laughs it off as a guess. He rationalizes it.

**Ch 5 — a dream fragment, not the bus dream**
Break the pattern: a *new* dream, brief, disorienting — not the bus, but something like a rooftop, or two hands letting go rather than holding on. He wakes without understanding it. Don't gloss it — let it sit unexplained. This is your first hint of the fall/dual-suicide without naming it.

**Ch 6 — the "asking out" scene, inverted from her letter**
Her letter tells us that in this life *he* was rejected once and stayed stubborn, and that she was surprised. Dramatize that here — but from his numb POV, so the reader doesn't yet know it echoes anything. Let his in-yeon cynicism from ch2 resurface: he almost sabotages it because he doesn't believe he's owed anything good (callback to bumo-eun/debt theme).

**Ch 7 — her strangeness**
A scene where she does something quietly out of character for a present-day teenager — knows a place, hums something, reacts oddly to an anniversary-feeling date, or flinches at heights/falling in a way that doesn't match her established fearlessness. He notices but doesn't push. This is the chapter where the *reader* should start suspecting she's the one carrying the memory, not him.

**Ch 8 — the bus dream returns, changed**
The dream comes back, but now with a detail that's clearly *her* memory leaking into *his* dream rather than the reverse (the two-directional bleed I mentioned last time). Maybe he dreams something he has no way of knowing — her dropping out of nursing school, a version of a bridge that isn't his. He wakes shaken and, for the first time, asks her something oblique about it. She deflects — gently, not coldly. This is her almost-telling-him-and-stopping moment.

**Ch 9 — his hobby fades**
Give the reader the turn the whole book has been building to: he stops going to the bridge. Not because he's "cured," but because his life has started to feel like it has weight again — mirrored explicitly against the ch1 utilitarian monologue ("a rounding error"). This is where his arc completes. It also raises the stakes, because the reader now knows — even if he doesn't — that the danger has effectively moved from him to her.

**Ch 10 — the clock, obliquely**
Something marks time without naming it — her talking about "this year" in a way that reads as ordinary until it isn't, in hindsight. Maybe a birthday, a countdown she brushes off as superstition. Keep it small. This is your last chance to seed the "expiration" before it happens, so it can't feel like a twist.

**Ch 11 — her death**
I'd keep this short, almost clinical, matching the compression of ch3 — a real tonal rupture from the warmth of ch9–10. Consider *not* showing the moment directly — he finds out after, the way we found out about the overcoat man in ch1 (nice existing motif: "people don't owe you their continued existence"). That line from ch1 could even echo back here almost verbatim, which would land hard.

**Ch 12 — the letter (epilogue)**
Now it's earned. I'd tighten it as discussed, and consider ending the book *on* the letter — no further narrator reflection after. Let "I'm sure I'll find you again" be the last line the reader sits with, same way ch2 ended on "it just felt like the beginning" — bookending the whole novel on that phrase's ironic return.

One more thing worth deciding before you draft: does he ever find out that in the old universe, *he* fell first — that her death was a lovers' suicide *following his*? If the letter is the only place this comes out, that's a huge, quiet gut-punch: he spends the whole book worried about his own death-wish endangering himself, never realizing he's the reason she died once already, and the reason she came back was to stop it from happening to him this time. That reframes his entire arc retroactively — worth protecting as the last reveal, even after the "second chance" reveal, if you want two gut-punches instead of one.
