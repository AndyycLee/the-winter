An oversized jacket. An unlit cigarette. One eye under a hood. A voice through falling snow.

Something in me rose at that.

Then didn't. The hood was bangs. The snow was only light, morning light, ordinary and undramatic, falling through.

She smiled. Small, a little uncertain, like she wasn't sure she had permission to take up the room she was standing in. But it was her eyes that stopped me. Wide, upturned, peeking out from under her bangs, they were a shade of aquamarine that looked lit from within. There was a pure, curious charm to them, the kind that made you want to follow the light back to its source. Yet they gave nothing away. Paired with her shyness, it made her magnetic.

It certainly resembled her. The one from the winter, but was my memory overlapping itself again? She seemed like an entirely different person.

And also — I'd seen her before. Years ago. Back when I couldn't speak English and spent recess in the library instead of outside, we'd shared class too. She was always quiet but bright, always surrounded by friends and laughter.

*They searched for blue their whole life through.*

My thoughts wouldn’t stop racing. Would she even recognize me? Did she remember any of it: the library, me? But deeper than that, a question I couldn't push down. The bus. The bench. The cold.

Then the room moved in around her, the way rooms always seemed to move around people who belonged in them. Names called out. A chair scraped over. Someone already asking her something and laughing before she'd even finished answering. She was folded into it almost instantly, swallowed into the current the way a stone disappears the moment it breaks the surface of a river, and I sat there twelve feet away, watching people who weren't me close a distance I hadn't even managed to attempt.

*Then passed right by — and never knew.*

I spent the rest of the day rehearsing sentences I never used.

By last period I'd assembled at least four different versions of "hey, did we—" and talked myself out of every one before it left my mouth. In the end I wasn't able to ask her a single thing. Not her name, not whether she remembered the library, not whether some part of her had ever stood on a bridge in the snow. The day just closed over her the same way it had opened. Cleanly. Without me in it.

I told myself I'd try something else instead.

That night I went back to the bridge. I'm not sure what I expected to find there. Some kind of instrument reading, maybe. A number that would tell me, conclusively, whether what I'd felt that morning — real, undeniable, mine — belonged to a person standing on cold concrete, or a person still asleep in his own bed.

The railing was the same railing. The water moved the same dirty blue-grey it always did. I put my hand where I'd put it before and waited to feel something I could trust.

Nothing came. Or something did, and I couldn't tell the difference between feeling it now and remembering having felt it, which amounted to the same failure either way. That was the part I hadn't planned for. That even standing in the exact spot, doing the exact thing, I had no instrument capable of measuring whether I was awake. A dream can borrow real fear the same as it can borrow a real railing. There was no version of this experiment that gave me an answer, because I was the one running it, and I was the one thing in it I couldn't verify.

It felt, briefly, like a very expensive way to learn nothing.

I stood there a while longer anyway, mostly out of stubbornness, watching my breath do the same slow work the snow had done in the dream. Going nowhere. Disappearing.

Then I turned around, half-expecting the joke to finish itself.

The bench was empty.

Just wet wood, and the shape the snow makes when nothing has sat in it for a long time.
