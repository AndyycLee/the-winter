## Twelve

An oversized jacket. An unlit cigarette. One eye under a hood. A voice through falling snow.

Then paused. The hood was bangs. The snow was only light, morning light, ordinary and undramatic, falling through.

Her smile was faint and uncertain, the kind worn by people who apologize just for existing. She stood just inside the doorway, looking around while the room rearranged itself around her.

Wide, upturned, eyes peeking out from under her bangs, a shade of aquamarine that looked lit from within. There was a candid, curious charm to them, the kind that made you want to follow the light back to its source. Yet they gave nothing away. Paired with her shyness, it made her intimidatingly magnetic.

I couldn't trust if my memories were overlapping, but my second take was winning. She seemed like an entirely different person.

And also — I'd seen her before. Years ago. Back when I couldn't speak English and spent recess in the library instead of outside, we'd shared class too. She was always quiet but bright, always surrounded by friends and laughter.

_They looked for one till years were done._

Still, my thoughts started to race. Would she even recognize me? Did she remember any of it: the library, me? But deeper than that, a question I couldn't push down. The bus. The bench. The cold.

Then the room moved in around her, the way rooms always seemed to move around people who belonged in them. Names called out. A chair scraped over. Someone already asking her something and laughing before she'd even finished answering. She was folded into it almost instantly, swallowed into the current the way a stone disappears the moment it breaks the surface, and I sat there twelve feet away, watching people who weren't me close a distance I hadn't even managed to attempt.

_Then stood right there and told no one._

◇

I spent the rest of the day rehearsing sentences I never used.

By last period I'd assembled three different versions of "hey, did we—" and talked myself out of every one before it left my mouth. The day just closed over her the same way it had opened. 

I told myself I'd try something else instead.

That night I went back to the bridge. I'm not sure what I expected to find there. I figured I could recreate the same conditions of the morning. If in-yeon was real. If she had come to school today. Then surely this could be real too.

The railing was the same railing. The water moved the same dirty blue-grey it always did. I put my hand where I'd put it before and looked down into the current the way I'd been looking when she spoke.

I gave it an hour. Then the hour after that, past the point where my fingers stopped reporting anything back, because leaving early felt like exactly the kind of small failure the universe would be within its rights to hold against me. Keep your eye on the thread. Step through the door with courage. My father's superstition. And here I was standing in the cold since midnight observing it like a prayer.

Everything was where it had been. And it didn't matter.

I stood there a while longer anyway, mostly out of stubbornness, watching my breath dissipate into the cold.

4:57 came and went.

Then I turned around, half-expecting the joke to finish itself.

The bench was empty.

Just rough wood, and the shape the snow makes when nothing has sat in it for a long time.

◇

I told myself I'd talk to her tomorrow. Tomorrow turned out to be the safest word I owned that week — infinitely renewable, never actually due.

I'll be honest. What I did next was probably overreaching.

I went to school. That's part of a whole confession, and I'm aware of how it lands. A boy attends class five days running, somebody call the papers. But you'd have to know what my attendance had looked like before. I was possessed.

Perfect attendance, homework in on time, because being a problem would have meant being looked at, and I couldn't afford to be looked at by anyone but her. I could not tell you one thing that happened in any of my own classes that week. I still have the notebook. It's blank.

I started taking the earlier bus. Not for a reason I could have defended out loud. She wasn't on it. I'd confirmed that by the second morning, which is a sentence I would like to walk quickly past. I just wanted to already be inside when she arrived, so that whatever happened would happen somewhere I'd gotten to first.

Which stairwell she came up. Or which door she used after lunch, and that she used it about four minutes after the bell. They became fixtures in my mind.

I called in sick to the store on Wednesday, which I had never done. Then again on Friday. The manager said take care of yourself, and I said yeah, and hung up quickly.

I was sleeping about six hours. 3 to 7 AM at the bridge, then the earlier bus. Weekends I stayed longer. By Thursday my face in the bathroom mirror had started to really resemble a ghost.

I counted the days from the one she walked in.

The second day she caught my eye and I looked down at a textbook I wasn't reading, and told myself it hadn't looked like retreat, even though from twelve feet away I hadn't fooled anyone, least of all myself.

The third day someone else's joke earned the version of her laugh I'd been drafting sentences all week just to hear, and I put my head down and told myself that was information, not injury.

The fourth day she said something to the girl beside her and glanced back toward me for exactly as long as it takes a glance to not become anything, and I spent the rest of the period rehearsing which of my three opening lines could survive being said out loud.

The fifth day I didn't even manage the glance.

Mr. Holt handed the essays back that afternoon. Mine had no grade on it. Just a check, and under it, in the same neat looping hand: _See me if you'd like to._ I read it maybe five times, counted the words, decided the "if" was doing something specific, and put it in my bag. I did not go see him.

By the end of it I'd lost count of the versions of myself I'd auditioned and discarded before they ever reached her. It didn't feel like rejection. It never had, with anyone. I knew that failure by heart. I'd catalogued it years ago and filed it under weather. The house with the lights on, and eventually you stop knocking.

This one had a gate in front of it. I never made it up the path to knock at all, and there was nothing to file that under but myself. The twelve feet never changed. Somehow that felt like widening anyway, the way a wound can ache worse for staying exactly the same size, day after day, instead of closing.

Tomorrow stopped being infinitely renewable on Friday afternoon, when the office taped a notice up by the stairwell. Last day of term the following Friday. Have a safe holiday.

Two weeks. She'd go wherever people like her go over a holiday and come back in January having had an entire life in the meantime, and by then a bench in the snow would be a month old and unaskable, and I'd be a boy in the back row who once looked at her strangely.

Which gave me five days.

◇

I used four of them.

The last day of term was a half-day, and the building knew it before the bell did. Teachers gave up around second period. People were signing the insides of each other's folders, trading nothing, laughing too loud in the stairwells the way you do when the building is about to stop mattering for a short while.

I hadn't slept properly in eleven days. I want to be accurate about what that does, because it isn't what people think. It doesn't make you brave. It makes the part of you that argues go quiet, and from the outside those look identical.

I spent those four days building the reassurance up like something I could stand behind. Worst case, she wasn't the girl. Worst case I'd been mistaken, and being mistaken isn't a crime, people mistake each other constantly. She'd say no, I'd say sorry, and the world would go on doing what it does. I said it on the bus. I said it in the stairwell with my hand on the rail. It was a good argument and I believed every word of it and it didn't help at all.

Years of being alone do something to a person that I don't think gets described honestly very often. It isn't sadness. It's closer to a muscle that wastes from disuse, until the thing everyone else does without thinking, walking up to somebody and opening your mouth, takes a kind of strength you no longer have any of. I wasn't shy. Shy is a personality. Whatever was wrong with me was closer to an injury, and I'd been carrying it so long I'd stopped calling it anything at all.

By Friday I didn't have anything left to hold it down with.

She went out through the gate with two other girls. They got ahead of her by a few steps at the corner, arguing about nothing.

"Hey," I said.

Not one of the three I'd written. I'd spent a week on them and what came out was hey, and then nothing for long enough that she had to fill it.

"Hi?" She turned, pleasant, a little quizzical. Up close her eyes were almost the blue I'd been carrying around for eleven days, and almost is a useless word. They were beautiful, the only thing I could be sure of. I looked away first.

"Sorry." I heard my own voice doing something strange, going too fast at the front of each sentence. "The abandoned bridge past the river. Were you there? A couple of weeks ago, early morning."

She pondered. She actually pondered it, which was somehow cruel.

"I don't think so," she said. "I don't really know where that is."

"You had a black jacket. Too big for you. And a cigarette you never lit." I couldn't stop.  "You asked me what I was doing and I said I was staring at the water."

"I don't smoke."

She said it gently. She said it the way you'd hand somebody back something they'd dropped.

"Okay," my voice trailing. "Okay..."

There was a pause where the next word should have been. An earbud. A shared silence. I stood there and swallowed them back in my throat.

"Were you okay?" she asked. "Out there. I mean."

It was the only thing she said all day that fit.

"Yeah," I said. "Sorry. I'm sorry..."

"It's okay." And then, because she was the kind of person who would: "I hope you find her."

Her friends called something from the corner. She said she'd see me in January, and she meant it kindly, and she went.

◇

I walked home instead of taking the bus, and by the time I reached my street I'd already built the other version, which took no effort at all. It was humiliating. It assembled itself in about six minutes.

I woke at 4:57 from a nightmare about falling. I did not go anywhere. I fell asleep again in the clothes I was already wearing, and the rest of it happened behind my eyes where it always happened. The girl on the bench was the girl from the bus dream in a different coat, because that is what dreams do, they recycle. In the morning my body did the route.

Come to think of it, I didn't remember standing up from her bench. Not the walk back, not the stop, not getting on.

Every piece fit. It had always fit. I'd just needed it not to.

I'd like to say I took it badly. What I actually felt, walking up the stairs to the apartment, was something closer to rest. Zero is a stable number. It doesn't ask you to do anything. For eleven days I'd been carrying around the idea that the universe had gone to some trouble on my behalf, and putting it down was easier than carrying it had been, and I understood then that this was the reason people like me stay people like me.

The thread hadn't snapped. My father's superstition needed a thread first.

I did go up to the second floor before I left the building that day. The lights in Mr. Holt's room were off and the door was locked, everyone gone early for the holiday, the chairs already up on the desks. Two weeks. It could wait two weeks.

I still have that paper. I never asked him what he wanted to say.
